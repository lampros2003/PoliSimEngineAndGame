## The sectors

| Sector | Holds | Covers |
|---|---|---|
| Households | Money (bank deposits) | Households and non-profit institutions serving them |
| Firms | Money, bank loans | Non-financial corporations |
| Banks | Gilts, loans; their deposits are everyone else's money | Financial corporations, including the Bank of England |
| Government | Gilts issued | The public sector, measured as public sector net borrowing |
| Rest of the world | Money, gilts | Everyone abroad |

## What happens each month

1. **Labour.** Public employment is the public pay bill divided by public pay per worker.
   Private employment moves a quarter of the way each month toward the number that firms'
   sales justify, as far as the workers not employed by the state allow (stage S11.3: a
   curve, `labour_capacity`, that fills every job wanted up to 95.5% of them and then ever
   fewer, never more than 98%). The rest of the labour force is unemployed, though never
   much less than 2% of it (`employment_capacity`), even when the state hires faster than
   firms let go.
2. **Income.** Wages, public pay, benefits (a fixed bill set by policy plus an amount per
   unemployed person), firms' payouts (dividends and self-employment income, raised when
   firms hold more cash than usual), interest on gilts, and taxes on income.
3. **Spending.** Households spend a fixed share of disposable income plus a small share of
   their money. Firms invest a fixed share of last month's sales. Exports follow world
   demand; imports are a fixed share of sales. VAT, other taxes on production and
   corporation tax follow their bases.
4. **Finance.** Government deficits are covered by selling gilts, to banks and abroad in
   fixed shares; surpluses buy them back. Firms borrow from banks whatever keeps their cash
   at its usual share of sales, and repay when they have more.
5. **Accounts.** GDP, unemployment, inflation and the debt ratios are computed.

Prices rise each month by target inflation plus a Phillips-curve term in the gap between
unemployment and its natural rate (stage S11.3: a curve, `phillips_shape`, straight within a
point of the natural rate, steeper a point at a time as the labour market tightens, half as
steep when it is slack). Pay per worker rises with prices and productivity,
and by a further point a year for each point unemployment is below its natural rate (a
wage curve; less when it is above), which is what keeps employment from running ahead of
the labour force. Amounts set by policy (spending bills, benefit rates) are in 2024
pounds and are uprated by a trend index that grows at target inflation plus trend real
growth, so unchanged policy means unchanged policy in real, per-head terms.

Ten **inputs** stand for what the model takes as given: the labour force beyond its
trend, world demand for exports, the market yield on new gilts, price and pay shocks,
and multipliers on the shares the model otherwise holds fixed (household spending out of
income, business investment, imports, the labour share). They are levers marked
`player = false`: scenarios set them and the game never shows them. At their defaults
they change nothing; the hindcast sets them month by month from published figures.

## The people

`population.toml` holds the UK's population by the 12 ITL1 regions, sex and single year of
age (0 to 89, and 90 and over): 2,184 cells, starting from ONS's mid-2024 estimates
(69,281,437 people). Each month:

- **deaths** follow the 2022-2024 national life table, scaled by region so that the
  first month is one twelfth of each region's deaths in the year to mid-2024;
- **births** follow an assumed bell-shaped profile of the mother's age (peak 31, spread
  5.5, ages 15 to 45), scaled by region to its births; 51.2% are boys;
- **net migration** (international and internal) arrives at each region's rate in the
  year to mid-2024, spread over ages by an assumed profile (peak 27, spread 9);
- one twelfth of each year of age has a birthday and moves up a year; 90 and over keeps
  its own.

The first month's births and deaths are exactly one twelfth of the published year, and a
year on the population has grown by close to the published year's growth. The shapes are
in `tools/uk/assumptions.toml` under `[population]`.

The economy does not read the people yet: the labour force is still the national series.
Linking them, and fertility, mortality and migration that change over time, belong to the
next stages.

## The industries

`industries.toml` splits the economy into 15 industries (SIC 2007 sections, aggregated
from the 104 products of ONS's 2023 input-output analytical tables; the list is in
`data/INDUSTRIES.md`). Each month households' spending, government spending (pay and
purchases), investment (business, dwellings, public) and exports are turned into demand
for UK products at the tables' shares, net of imports and product taxes. Each industry's
output is then the Leontief inverse applied to that demand: what it sells to final buyers
plus what it sells to other industries along the way. Value added and pay by industry are
the tables' shares of output.

In the first month the industries' value added is within 1% of the tables' value added
scaled to 2024, and every industry's output within 4% of its 2023 output scaled the same
way. Cutting government purchases falls mostly on public administration, education and
health, as it should. The economy does not read the industries back yet: prices and
imports by industry are later work.

### Jobs by industry (stage S6.4)

`tools/uk/jobs.py` takes 2024's workforce jobs by industry from ONS's JOBS02 (the average
of the four quarters, seasonally adjusted; `data/industry_jobs.csv`): employee jobs,
self-employment jobs, HM Forces and trainees, 36.7 million in all. The model's people in
work (33.6 million, from the Labour Force Survey) times 1.092 workforce jobs per person in
work (second jobs, and the two surveys' difference) gives the workforce jobs, and each
industry has a share of them: in the first month its 2024 share, so every industry starts
at its 2024 jobs exactly. Each month after, an industry's share moves with its output's
growth against the other industries': by half of it (`jobs_output_elasticity`, low 0.3 and
high 0.7), because output per worker grows faster where output grows faster (Verdoorn's
law), and the shares are scaled back to add to one. The economy's employment still comes
from the labour block; the industries only share it out.

Pay per employee job by industry is the industry's compensation of employees (pay with
employers' contributions, at the input-output tables' share of output) over its employee
jobs (JOBS02's employee jobs per workforce job): £43,000 a year across the economy in the
first month, from £23,000 in agriculture to £86,000 in finance.

With unchanged policy the jobs grow about 2.5% in five years in every industry, a little
faster in public services. Cutting every public service by a quarter (stage S8.0's
levers) takes about 650,000 jobs out of public administration, education and health within
two years, while the other industries gain about 290,000 and unemployment is about a point
higher. Both show on the "Who gains, who loses" panel, with their national totals.

## The households

`households.toml` runs the 2024-25 tax and benefit rules on the 20,000 synthetic households
in `households/` every month (see `households/README.md` for how they are built and what
the rules cover). Their pay moves with the economy's pay per worker and their pensions with
the trend index; every money threshold and benefit amount rises with prices, so unchanged
policy keeps their real value. The economy reads them back:

- **income tax** is the households' income tax plus tax on the economy's other income
  (savings, dividends, property, the self-employed the records miss) at the rate that makes
  the base month one twelfth of 2024;
- **National Insurance** is the households' contributions, scaled so the base month
  matches;
- **Child Benefit and Universal Credit** come from the households; the "pensions and other
  benefits" lever holds the rest.

So the player's tax levers are the real ones: the personal allowance, every rate and
threshold, Child Benefit and Universal Credit amounts, grouped in the policy panel.

From the second month the households' weights are raked each month (stage S4.4) so that
they match households by type, people in households by region, sex and age band (the
cohort table above, times the base year's share living in households), and people in work
(the economy's employment). So the households age with the population, and when
employment falls, households without work count for more: Universal Credit rises and
income tax falls with it. Their pay follows the economy's pay per worker.

## Spending by function (stage S8.0)

The public pay bill and purchases of goods and services are split into nine functions
at their 2024-25 shares in HM Treasury's Public Expenditure Statistical Analyses 2025
(OGL; `tools/uk/functions.py`, `data/functions.csv`): health, education, defence,
police, courts and prisons, transport, social care, environment and housing, culture, and
the rest of government and the economy. Each is a lever, in 2024 prices, in the policy
panel's "Public services" group, and a bill like any spending. A function's pay and
purchases keep their 2024 split (`muldiv`, so the default settings give the 2024 totals
to the penny), and the economy reads their sums, the public pay bill and purchases, as it
did when they were levers: public employment follows the pay bill.

PESA reports procurement by function, but pay only in total: pay by function is a
function's current spending less its procurement and the other spending that belongs to
it (debt interest and grants abroad in general services, subsidies in economic affairs,
benefits in social protection), scaled to PESA's pay total. Social care comes out as
purchases only, which is close: most of it is bought from private providers.

Each function's demand reaches the industries through its own mix of products, a judgement
raked so that together they buy the input-output tables' government purchases, so the
base month is unchanged. Cutting health spending by a third falls on the health industry
and on public employment, and brings a recession at once.

### What services deliver (stage S8.1)

Each function has a service level: its real spending (the lever, in 2024 prices) per
person it serves, against the first month, followed over about six months
(`service_adjust_months`, presets 3 and 12), since staff are hired and let go and
contracts run out over months. Health serves the population weighted by need (a
judgement shaped like the OBR's profile of health spending by age: people over 85 count
four and a half times someone of 45 to 64, children of 5 to 14 half), education people
aged 4 to 21, social care people aged 65 and over, and the rest everyone. With unchanged
money the levels wear down as the population grows and ages, health and social care
fastest: health falls below 95%, the threshold of a situation, in the 55th month. A
quarter off health spending takes its level halfway to 75% in about four months.

### The NHS waiting list (stage S9.0)

`tools/uk/nhs.py` reads NHS England's referral-to-treatment overview series (OGL;
`data/nhs_rtt.csv`, every month from August 2007 to January 2025): the number waiting for
planned hospital treatment in England, with NHS England's estimates for trusts that did
not report, the new referrals and the waits that ended in treatment. In 2024 about 1.74
million referrals joined the list a month, 1.50 million treatments left it, and about 3.3%
of the list a month left it for other reasons (validation, treatment elsewhere, patients
who no longer needed it), the gap between the flows and the change in the list, which fell
from 7.58 to 7.46 million. The longest list on record was 7.77 million, in September 2023.

In the model (`nhs.toml`) the list starts at 7.46 million, December 2024's. Referrals grow
with the population weighted by its need for health care (S8.1); treatments follow real
health spending as the service has adjusted to it (`treatment_response`: 10% more real
spending treats 10% more patients, a judgement, presets 0.5 and 1.5; since stage S11.3 along
a curve, `treatment_curve`: a cut bites a fifth harder, and rises beyond a fifth buy ever
fewer treatments); other removals stay
at 2024's share of the list. With unchanged money referrals grow about 1.3% a year, as the
population grows and ages, while treatments stay flat, so the list passes its record in the
30th month and reaches 8.8 million by the 2029 election. A tenth off health spending leaves
it about 2 million longer than unchanged money after two years. The list is
in the headline; "Months to clear the waiting list" divides it by this month's treatments
(about five).

## Emissions (stage S8.2)

`tools/uk/emissions.py` takes 2024's greenhouse gas emissions from ONS's atmospheric
emissions accounts (UK residence basis, OGL; `data/emissions.csv`): 478 million tonnes of
carbon dioxide equivalent, 354 million from the industries (the most from energy and
water, transport, manufacturing and agriculture) and 124 million from households' own
heating and driving. In the first month every industry emits its 2024 tonnes; after that
in proportion to its output at constant prices, and households in proportion to their
spending, times an intensity that falls about 3% a year (`intensity_fall`, presets 2% and
4%: the accounts' emissions fell from 821 to 478 million tonnes from 1990 to 2024 while
output nearly doubled).

A carbon price (the "Environment" group, pounds a tonne, a bill) speeds the fall, by 2% a
year more at £100 a tonne (`carbon_price_response`, a judgement, presets 1% and 3%), and
firms and households pay it to the government on the previous month's emissions, as
public receipts: £100 a tonne raises about £48bn a year at first. Since stage S9.3 it
also raises consumer prices (below): households pay their own share in full and firms pass
on some of theirs.

With unchanged policy emissions fall about 1.5% a year and move more than 5% above a
straight path from 2024's to nothing in 2050 in the 16th month; at £100 a tonne they fall
about 3.5% a year, still above the path, which falls by a twenty-sixth of 2024's each
year.

## Consumer prices (stage S9.3)

The price level, `price_index`, is the GDP deflator and follows the Phillips curve.
`prices.toml` adds consumer prices: the deflator times one plus a `tax_wedge`, what taxes
on spending put on shop prices. The wedge heads for

- the VAT pass-through share (`vat_pass_through`, 0.9, presets 0.6 and 1.0: most of the
  2008 cut and the 2010 and 2011 rises reached prices) of the rise in what VAT adds to
  prices since the first month (one plus the rate times the share of spending it falls on);
- plus the share of firms' carbon price and customs duties they pass on
  (`cost_pass_through`, 0.6, presets 0.3 and 0.9), over all private demand;
- plus households' own carbon price, over their spending, in full;

and gets there over about three months (`price_adjust_months`, presets 1 and 6). All of
them are judgements. Consumer inflation (`consumer_inflation_annual`) is the deflator's
plus the wedge's growth, averaged over about a year. Households' real incomes, on which
voters judge the government, are their incomes over consumer prices, and voters weigh
consumer inflation since the last election; household emissions follow spending at
consumer prices; and private pay makes up half of consumer prices' rise above the
deflator (`wage_catchup`, presets 0.25 and 0.75). In the first month every rate is
2024's, so the wedge is nil and the base month is unchanged.

A year after a 2.5-point VAT rise consumer prices are 1.3% above the deflator, consumer
inflation is 0.4 points higher, and the government's poll rating is about a point lower
than with unchanged policy; a £100 carbon price does much the same (1.2%) and cuts
households' emissions 3% more; a 20% barrier on China adds 0.14%. The deflator itself does
not rise: higher taxes on spending weigh on firms' sales, and the Phillips curve sees
only unemployment, so a second round of inflation through wages and prices is not
modelled.

With policy unchanged, debt passes 100% of GDP in the 18th month, emissions move above
their path in the 16th, the waiting list passes its record in the 30th and the health
service falls below 95% in the 55th. Stage S13.7's six (the indicators of `policies.toml`) are
off with policy unchanged, which by the 2029 election raises crime 1.3% and takes 0.16 years
off healthy life; a tenth off the police, very restrictive planning, a £20,000 or £60,000 visa
threshold, or very liberal planning with a social housing programme each turn one on. Cutting purchases of goods and services by a quarter brings a recession at
once and high unemployment from the sixth month, both over within two years. (That was
written when purchases were one lever; with spending by function, cutting health spending by
a third does the same, with unemployment above 6% at once.)

## Dilemmas (stage S9.2)

opens when its start holds, waits for an answer, and each answer changes formulas the model
already has). Every size is a judgement. Costs are shares of an existing payment: 1% of
procurement is about £3.3bn a year, of public investment £1.4bn, of subsidies £0.4bn, of
public pay £2.9bn, of benefits £3.1bn. What an answer does to voters goes through
`government_standing` (politics.toml), by voter type: a standing of -0.02 weighs on a voter
type as much as about a third of a point more unemployment in its region, or ten months
more in office.

The chances draw from random streams of their own, so a game's seed decides when they come:
with the 2025 scenario's seed and policy unchanged, nine open in five years (the pay claim
in the sixth month, the steelworks in the eleventh, the summit, prisons and net zero in the
seventeenth and eighteenth, the chip maker in the nineteenth, energy prices in the twenty-
sixth, the NHS winter crisis in the thirtieth and floods in the thirty-second); the bank
needs a slump. Since stage S13.7, answering each with its first answer, thirteen come by
the July 2029 election: those nine, prisons again in June 2028, fuel protests in January
2028, small boats in March 2029 and the NHS winter crisis again in July 2029. `crates/core/tests/uk_dilemmas.rs` gives every answer and checks that each
element it names moves the way its text says.

## The public finances (stage S13.8)

`finances.toml` gives the lenders' side. Borrowing as a share of GDP is averaged over about a
year (`borrowing_smoothed`); lenders take its level in the first month as normal
(`borrowing_at_start`), and for each point of GDP it runs above that they add
`lender_premium_effect` points to the yield on new gilts, and take off as much below it,
within a premium of two points and a discount of half a point (`gilt_premium`). The yield on
new gilts with the premium is `gilt_yield_now`, an indicator on the board; the average rate
on the debt follows it over the gilts' fourteen-year life (`effective_rate`, model.toml), so
a premium reaches debt interest slowly, and through the gilt interest banks pass on,
households' incomes. Since stage S14.5 it also reaches new mortgages, and so home owners'
incomes and votes, and firms' investment (below, "Interest rates"). `lender_premium_effect` is nil in the model
and 0.75 in the 2025 scenario's `[params]`; the hindcast model has no `finances.toml` and
uses the yield as before. Doing nothing raises smoothed borrowing about half a point and the
yield from 4.2% to 4.6% by 2029; the crowd-pleaser's 1.2 points take it past 5%.

What voters make of borrowing is in `politics/README.md`, "The public finances".

## Interest rates (stage S14.5)

`finances.toml` also holds the rates households and firms pay. Every term is the change from
the base month's rates, 2024's, so at those rates the 2024 accounts are reproduced exactly;
none is in the hindcast model, which has no `finances.toml` (as the lenders' premium is
not).

- **Bank Rate** starts at 4.75%, in force from November 2024 to February 2025. Each month it
  moves a sixth of the way to a rule: a neutral rate, plus 1.5 points for each point consumer
  inflation runs above 2% and a point for each point unemployment is below its natural rate
  (Taylor's weights, the output gap read through Okun's law), within 0.1% and 15%. The neutral
  rate is whatever makes the rule give 4.75% at the base month's inflation and unemployment.
- **The gilt yield** on new gilts moves half a point for each point Bank Rate is above 4.75%
  (a judgement: from 2021 to 2023 Bank Rate rose 5.15 points and ten-year yields about 3.5),
  on top of the lenders' premium. A fifth of the debt, the Bank's gilts bought with reserves
  (about £560bn), pays Bank Rate at once (`reserves_interest`, in debt interest to UK holders).
- **The mortgage rate** on all mortgages starts at about 3.8% (the Bank of England's effective
  rate on outstanding secured lending, December 2024) and follows the rate on new mortgages
  over about two and a half years, as fixed deals end (about 85% are fixed, mostly for two or
  five years). New mortgages move by half Bank Rate's change and half the gilt yield's, with
  the lenders' premium. On the £1.65 trillion of mortgages (Bank of England, lending secured
  on dwellings) households pay the change in the rate (`mortgage_interest_rise` and
  `mortgage_interest_fall`), which comes off their disposable income.
- **Households' mortgages** are spread over the synthetic households by the age of the first
  adult (8% of households under 25 have one, 38% at 25 to 34, 52% at 35 to 49, 36% at 50 to 64
  and 5% over 65; English Housing Survey 2023-24, rounded) and in proportion to income, scaled
  to the stock (`mortgage_per_income`, about 3.2 times income for a household with a share of
  one). The change in each household's payments comes off the real income its voters judge
  (`politics/README.md`).
- **Firms** pay Bank Rate's change on their bank loans (mostly floating), and **business
  investment** falls 4% for each point the real cost of borrowing (half Bank Rate, half the
  gilt yield, less consumer inflation), averaged over a year, runs above the base month's (a
  judgement: a user-cost elasticity of about a half on a cost of capital of about 12%).

Doing nothing, Bank Rate drifts down to about 3.9% by 2029 as unemployment rises to 5%, and
the mortgage rate to about 3.7%. The crowd-pleaser's borrowing raises Bank Rate to 5.3% and
the mortgage rate to 4.1% by 2027; a slump cuts Bank Rate, and the wrecker's takes it to the
floor. Bank Rate and the mortgage rate are indicators in Economy, neither good nor bad.

## Slumps that last (stage S14.5)

`labour.toml` makes a slump outlast its cause (none of it is in the hindcast model):

- **Labour hoarding.** Firms close a gap in their staff at a quarter a month when they want
  more (`hiring_speed`, as before) and at 8% a month when they want fewer (`firing_speed`, a
  judgement: in 2008-09 output fell about 6% and employment 2%, and kept falling into 2010).
  The state does the same as its pay bill moves (`public_employment_held`), so a cut to a
  service no longer puts its staff out of work in one month.
- **Long unemployment.** Unemployment above the natural rate, averaged over a year, raises the
  natural rate by half as much (`hysteresis_effect`, a judgement after Ball, 2009): the
  long-term unemployed restrain pay less, so pay, prices and Bank Rate read a tighter market.
- **Slow pay cuts.** Above the natural rate, pay falls behind prices a quarter as fast as it
  rises ahead of them below it (`wage_shape`, a judgement: Elsby, Shin and Solon, 2016).

- **Slow rehiring.** Firms hire at `hiring_speed` over one plus `rehiring_drag` (100) times
  long unemployment: each point of it halves the speed (the long-term unemployed are slower
  to place). Long unemployment counts for at most three points (`long_unemployment_most`).

After a year of business investment a quarter down, unemployment peaks two months after
output's trough and two years on is still 4.7%, against 4.3% at the start. The wrecker's
unemployment no longer heals: it is 15.6% at the 2029 election, against 7.5% after S13.8.

## Homelessness and productivity (stage S14.5)

`statistics.toml` holds two indicators the owner asked for; their sources and sizes are in
`POLICIES.md`. **Homeless households** (Housing, a bad thing) are the households in temporary
accommodation in England, 126,040 on 30 September 2024: fewer with rent controls, more homes
built and the social housing programme; more with working-age poverty, unemployment and dearer
mortgages, over about a year. **Productivity** (Economy, a good thing) is output per job beyond
its trend, 2024 = 100: it follows business and public investment against their trend, school
results and healthy life over about five years, and private pay and exports follow it. Both
are at their 2024 levels in the base month; doing nothing, homelessness rises to about 132,000
by 2029 as unemployment rises, and productivity stays within 0.1 of 100.

## Hindcast, 2005 to 2024

`tools/uk/hindcast.py` calibrates the model to 2004, runs it monthly from the middle of
2004 to the end of 2024 with the published policy, labour force, exports and gilt yields,
and compares each year with the outturn. The bands were set in `docs/S3-PLAN.md` before
any run. The full tables are in `hindcast/REPORT.md`.

| Band | Run B (main) | Run A (own behaviour) | Run C (B, prices and pay as published) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nominal GDP within 5% every year | **Met** (largest +4.5%, 2012) | Missed in 17 years (up to +17%) | Met (largest +4.4%) |
| Borrowing within 2 points of GDP every year | Missed in 3 years: 2015, 2019, 2021 (up to +2.6) | Missed in 12 years (up to −6.2) | Missed in the same 3 years |
| Unemployment within 1.5 points every year | Missed in 8 years: 2006-07, 2010-13, 2022-23 (up to −3.1) | Missed in 8 years (up to −4.7) | Missed in 5 years, 2009-2013 (up to −2.3) |
| Net debt within 10 points of GDP at the end of 2014 | **Met** (−6.6; −1.6 against debt built from borrowing) | Missed (−33.5) | Met (−6.4) |

Reading the misses:

- **Run A against B** is the cost of v0's fixed shares. The model has no financial
  sector, no pandemic and no exchange rate, so the 2008-09 crisis (household saving up
  three points, business investment's share of sales down by almost a third) and the 2020 lockdowns cannot come
  from inside it; with those shares following the published figures, GDP and debt are
  inside their bands.
- **Borrowing** misses in 2015, 2019 and 2021 by 2.2 to 2.6 points; debt interest
  accounts for 1.1 to 1.4 of each (no Bank of England, above). Receipts are within one
  point of GDP on average.
- **Unemployment** is too low before the crisis (2006-07, when wages in the model lag
  the published pay rises), during the slow recovery (2010-13, no labour hoarding) and
  in 2022-23 (prices too low, so real demand too high: with prices as published, run C,
  this miss goes). Its average error is 1.2 points in run B and 1.0 in run C.
- **The wage curve** was added because of the hindcast: without it, run A's average
  unemployment error was 2.9 points and a spending boom could drive unemployment to
  zero. With it the 2025 baseline settles at 4.6%, beside the natural rate of 4.5%.

## The base year

The first month reproduces, as one twelfth of the 2024 figures: household and non-profit

£1,415.9bn, public sector current receipts £1,121.3bn, net borrowing £148.6bn, GDP

## The statistics audit (stage S15.2)

`python tools/uk/audit.py` moves each of the board's policies one step and reports, for every
statistic on the board, how many move it noticeably within a year and what it feeds
(`STATISTICS.md`). Run on the model before stage S15.2, it settled `docs/S15-PLAN.md`'s list:

- **Left the board** (the rows stay in the model and the popups), as planned: tax receipts (moved
  by 11 policies, but only a step toward the budget balance), exports and imports (the trade
  balance replaces them), the housing stock (9, but homes built says it), NHS treatments (only
  the NHS moves it, and only toward the waiting list), jobs (7, feeds nothing), household income
  (7, feeds nothing; median income stays), working-age poverty (feeds only homelessness) and
  homes' emissions (part of emissions).
- **The edge cases, kept.** Productivity and healthy life move by less than 1% in a year
  under any one policy, but they move over a term and feed pay, output and a situation.
  Alcohol deaths, net migration, smoking and the prison population each move under one policy
  only and feed little, but each is the only place that policy shows. Pay per job moves under
  one policy and feeds nothing; it stays, as the one figure for pay.
- After stage S15.2 the board holds 41 statistics and 40 policies in force (55 with the 15 not
  in force). Rents and house prices move by under 1% in a year under one step of any policy
  (they adjust over years), and the tax burden and the trade balance feed nothing: they are on
  the board because the new groups and policies are about them.

Situations name the statistic that sets them off in `[play.web]` (`situations`), since the
model format takes no other key in a `[situation]` table.
