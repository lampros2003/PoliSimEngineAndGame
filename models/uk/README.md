# The United Kingdom, version 0

A monthly stock-flow model of the UK economy and public finances in model format 0
(`docs/spec/01-model-format-v0.md`), calibrated so that its first month is one twelfth of
2024. Milestone S3.2 of `docs/S3-PLAN.md`.

| File | What it holds | Edit it? |
|---|---|---|
| `model.toml` | Sectors, instruments, accounts and the slow-moving states (prices, pay, labour force, the average rate on debt, private employment) | Generated from `tools/uk/template/model.toml` |
| `economy.toml` | The month's flows and derived values, phase by phase | Generated from `tools/uk/template/economy.toml` |
| `calibration.toml` | Every parameter and lever setting, each with the figure it came from | Generated by `tools/uk/calibrate.py` |
| `data/` | The published series the calibration reads, with their sources (`MANIFEST.toml`) and identity checks (`CHECKS.md`) | Generated by `tools/uk/pipeline.py` |
| `scenarios/2025.toml` | The playable scenario: a five-year term from January 2025 | By hand |
| `golden/2025.hashes.csv` | The oracle's state hash for each of the first 120 months | Regenerated when the model changes (command below) |
| `hindcast/` | The 2005 to 2024 hindcast: `REPORT.md` and `results.csv` | Generated by `python tools/uk/hindcast.py` |

To change a judgement, edit `tools/uk/assumptions.toml`, then:

```bash
python tools/uk/calibrate.py
```

```bash
cd oracle && python -m polisim_oracle run ../models/uk/scenarios/2025.toml --steps 120 --columns step,hash --out ../models/uk/golden/2025.hashes.csv
```

The second command records the new state hashes that the oracle and the engine are both
tested against (`oracle/tests/test_uk.py`, `crates/core/tests/golden.rs`).

## The sectors

| Sector | Holds | Covers |
|---|---|---|
| Households | Money (bank deposits) | Households and non-profit institutions serving them |
| Firms | Money, bank loans | Non-financial corporations |
| Banks | Gilts, loans; their deposits are everyone else's money | Financial corporations, including the Bank of England |
| Government | Gilts issued | The public sector, measured as public sector net borrowing |
| Rest of the world | Money, gilts | Everyone abroad |

## What happens each month

1. **Labour.** Public employment is the public pay bill divided by public pay per worker.
   Private employment moves a quarter of the way each month toward the number that firms'
   sales justify. The rest of the labour force is unemployed.
2. **Income.** Wages, public pay, benefits (a fixed bill set by policy plus an amount per
   unemployed person), firms' payouts (dividends and self-employment income, raised when
   firms hold more cash than usual), interest on gilts, and taxes on income.
3. **Spending.** Households spend a fixed share of disposable income plus a small share of
   their money. Firms invest a fixed share of last month's sales. Exports follow world
   demand; imports are a fixed share of sales. VAT, other taxes on production and
   corporation tax follow their bases.
4. **Finance.** Government deficits are covered by selling gilts, to banks and abroad in
   fixed shares; surpluses buy them back. Firms borrow from banks whatever keeps their cash
   at its usual share of sales, and repay when they have more.
5. **Accounts.** GDP, unemployment, inflation and the debt ratios are computed.

Prices rise each month by target inflation plus a Phillips-curve term in the gap between
unemployment and its natural rate. Pay per worker rises with prices and productivity,
and by a further point a year for each point unemployment is below its natural rate (a
wage curve; less when it is above), which is what keeps employment from running ahead of
the labour force. Amounts set by policy (spending bills, benefit rates) are in 2024
pounds and are uprated by a trend index that grows at target inflation plus trend real
growth, so unchanged policy means unchanged policy in real, per-head terms.

Ten **inputs** stand for what the model takes as given: the labour force beyond its
trend, world demand for exports, the market yield on new gilts, price and pay shocks,
and multipliers on the shares the model otherwise holds fixed (household spending out of
income, business investment, imports, the labour share). They are levers marked
`player = false`: scenarios set them and the game never shows them. At their defaults
they change nothing; the hindcast sets them month by month from published figures.

## Known weaknesses

These are what version 0 does not do. The hindcast (below) shows which of them matter.

| Weakness | Effect | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Firms pay no interest on loans, and their payouts do not respond to their debt | Firms' borrowing in 2024 (2.2% of GDP, see below) continues for ever: loans rise from 17% of GDP to 62% in 30 years, with no effect on anything else | Interest on loans, and payouts that fall as debt rises relative to sales |
| No fiscal rule and no monetary policy | With unchanged policy the deficit drifts from 5.1% to 7.5% of GDP and debt from 97% to 153% in 30 years, because the interest rate on debt (about 4.3%) exceeds nominal growth (about 3.6%) while there is a primary deficit. That is the arithmetic of unchanged policy, not an error, but no government would let it run | A Bank Rate lever and a policy rule for the Bank of England; the gilt yield responding to Bank Rate and debt |
| Government is the public sector on the PSNB basis | Its 2024 borrowing is £148.6bn; general government in the sector accounts borrowed £174.2bn. Firms' net lending absorbs the £25.7bn difference (−£63.4bn in the model against −£36.0bn published) | Public corporations as a sector of their own, or a reconciliation line |
| Taxes are average rates on broad bases | Income tax, NICs, VAT and corporation tax are right in total but have no thresholds or bands, so a change to them has no distributional effect | Household records (stage S4) |
| Fixed shares for investment, imports, consumption and the labour share | No response to interest rates, prices or confidence. The hindcast's run A, where they stay fixed, misses GDP by up to 17% (2020) and borrowing by up to 6 points; with them following the published figures (run B) GDP is within 4.5% every year | Behavioural equations, S3.6 and later |
| No Bank of England | Under quantitative easing about a third of gilts were in effect financed at Bank Rate; the model pays gilt rates on all of them, so debt interest is 1 to 1.8 points of GDP too high in 2015-2021 and too low in 2022-2024 (when Bank Rate rose, and index-linked gilts paid RPI). This is most of the hindcast's borrowing miss | The central bank as a sector, Bank Rate as a lever, index-linked gilts |
| Employment moves with output within months | Firms kept staff through 2009-2013 and the model sheds them more slowly than output falls only through its hiring speed, so unemployment is 2 to 3 points too low then | Labour hoarding: hiring and firing at different speeds |
| No job retention schemes | In 2020 furlough is paid (as subsidies) but does not keep anyone in work | A job retention lever |
| Prices come only from the labour market | No energy or import prices: in 2022-2024 the model's prices end 10% below the published deflator unless shocked | Import prices and the exchange rate |
| No financial transactions | Debt grows only by borrowing; published debt also grew by loans to banks and students and asset purchases, about 5 points of GDP by 2014 and 8 by 2024 | Government lending as a flow with a claim instrument |
| No exchange rate | Exports follow world demand; imports a fixed share of sales | Later stage |
| Opening money holdings are round figures | Household deposits £1.9 trillion, firms' £0.8 trillion, foreign £0.5 trillion: orders of magnitude, not yet from the Bank of England's money and credit data | Add those series to the pipeline |
| Households' new dwellings are taken as 21% of whole-economy investment | Business investment is an estimate (£286.7bn) | The ONS business investment release |

## Hindcast, 2005 to 2024

`tools/uk/hindcast.py` calibrates the model to 2004, runs it monthly from the middle of
2004 to the end of 2024 with the published policy, labour force, exports and gilt yields,
and compares each year with the outturn. The bands were set in `docs/S3-PLAN.md` before
any run. The full tables are in `hindcast/REPORT.md`.

| Band | Run B (main) | Run A (own behaviour) | Run C (B, prices and pay as published) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nominal GDP within 5% every year | **Met** (largest +4.5%, 2012) | Missed in 17 years (up to +17%) | Met (largest +4.4%) |
| Borrowing within 2 points of GDP every year | Missed in 3 years: 2015, 2019, 2021 (up to +2.6) | Missed in 12 years (up to −6.2) | Missed in the same 3 years |
| Unemployment within 1.5 points every year | Missed in 8 years: 2006-07, 2010-13, 2022-23 (up to −3.1) | Missed in 8 years (up to −4.7) | Missed in 5 years, 2009-2013 (up to −2.3) |
| Net debt within 10 points of GDP at the end of 2014 | **Met** (−6.6; −1.6 against debt built from borrowing) | Missed (−33.5) | Met (−6.4) |

Reading the misses:

- **Run A against B** is the cost of v0's fixed shares. The model has no financial
  sector, no pandemic and no exchange rate, so the 2008-09 crisis (household saving up
  three points, business investment's share of sales down by almost a third) and the 2020 lockdowns cannot come
  from inside it; with those shares following the published figures, GDP and debt are
  inside their bands.
- **Borrowing** misses in 2015, 2019 and 2021 by 2.2 to 2.6 points; debt interest
  accounts for 1.1 to 1.4 of each (no Bank of England, above). Receipts are within one
  point of GDP on average.
- **Unemployment** is too low before the crisis (2006-07, when wages in the model lag
  the published pay rises), during the slow recovery (2010-13, no labour hoarding) and
  in 2022-23 (prices too low, so real demand too high: with prices as published, run C,
  this miss goes). Its average error is 1.2 points in run B and 1.0 in run C.
- **The wage curve** was added because of the hindcast: without it, run A's average
  unemployment error was 2.9 points and a spending boom could drive unemployment to
  zero. With it the 2025 baseline settles at 4.6%, beside the natural rate of 4.5%.

## The base year

The first month reproduces, as one twelfth of the 2024 figures: household and non-profit
consumption £1,766.5bn, disposable income £1,867.3bn, compensation of employees
£1,415.9bn, public sector current receipts £1,121.3bn, net borrowing £148.6bn, GDP
£2,904.9bn, exports £882.4bn and imports £903.5bn, unemployment 4.3% and net debt of
£2,816.4bn (97% of GDP). The first month is January 2025, so prices have already risen by
one month of inflation; matches are within half a percent.
