## 1. Cohort-component demographic projection (and multistate extensions) versus microsimulation

### Takeaway
Cohort-component projection (CCM) is the universal official method (UN WPP, Eurostat EUROPOP, national offices): cheap, deterministic and exact for age x sex (x region), but it is documented as limited to a small number of characteristics and cannot track individuals; official practice when many attributes are needed is to switch to microsimulation that reproduces the cohort-component totals.

### Cited Findings
- UN World Population Prospects 2024 is a cohort-component reconstruction and projection: an "internally consistent" annual population reconstruction 1950-2023 by age and sex with mortality, fertility and migration components for all countries/areas; methodology report is UN DESA/POP/2024/DC/NO.10 (July 2024) — [UN WPP 2024 Methodology](https://population.un.org/WPP/Publications/Files/WPP2024_Methodology.pdf); [Gerland, UN Population Division slides, Feb 2025](https://www.unescwa.org/sites/default/files/event/materials/Gerland_20250226_Insights%20from%20WPP-Estimation%20methodologies.pdf)
- Mechanics: the population by age and sex in a given year is projected forward by applying age- and sex-specific mortality, fertility and migration patterns — [Free Range Statistics, "Reproducing and adapting the UN Population Projections", 2024](https://freerangestats.info/blog/2024/05/05/pop-projections)
- WPP 2024 introduced a probabilistic model for projecting net international migration for all countries (per search summary; confirm in the methodology report) — [UN WPP 2024 Methodology report page](https://un.org/development/desa/pd/content/world-population-prospects-2024-methodology-report)
- Eurostat EUROPOP2023: deterministic "what-if" cohort-component projections for 30 countries (EU-27 + Iceland, Norway, Switzerland), base population 1 January 2022, a partial-convergence baseline, with four separate models for fertility, mortality, immigration and emigration; migration is described as the hardest component — [Eurostat, Population projections in the EU - methodology](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php/Population_projections_in_the_EU_-_methodology); [EUROPOP2023 metadata](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/cache/metadata/en/proj_23n_esms.htm)
- Regional (NUTS 3) Eurostat projections appear to exist only on the EUROPOP2019 basis; a 2026 research paper uses EUROPOP2019 for NUTS 3 fertility/mortality/migration and EUROPOP2023 only for national totals (secondary source; verify against Eurostat) — [arXiv 2603.26525](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.26525)
- Limits: cohort-component methods are "limited to a small number of characteristics"; they start from an age/sex distribution and cannot track individuals over time, whereas microsimulation starts from a micro-data population. Macro projection models "can be implemented as micro-simulations, producing identical results, but allowing for model extensions beyond the technical limitations of cohort-component models" — [IHSN, DYNAMIS-POP documentation, Chapter 1 (2017)](https://documentation.ihsn.org/dynamis/DYNAMIS-POP-MRT-2017_v01_01_Chapter1.html)
- The microsimulation-for-projection line of work is traced to van Imhoff & Post (1998), "Microsimulation methods for population projection", Population — [article listing](https://www.youscribe.com/catalogue/documents/savoirs/sciences-humaines-et-sociales/microsimulation-methods-for-population-projection-article-n-1-1078916)
- Marois & KC (SpringerBriefs in Population Studies, 2021) rebuild a multistate (age x sex x education x region) projection of India as a microsimulation and compare outputs — [Springer open-access book](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-030-79111-7.pdf); [chapter "Converting a Cohort Component Model into a Microsimulation Model"](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-79111-7_3)
- A 2023 IJM paper (Bacon et al., per search summary) reports that a simplified microsimulation design can diverge from cohort-component benchmarks by "varying magnitudes", as large as 20% in India (attribution of this URL to that paper is by best match) — [International Journal of Microsimulation, article 00289](https://microsimulation.pub/articles/00289)
- Statistics Canada's official ethnocultural/regional projections use microsimulation (Demosim) rather than CCM: the base population is the 20% census sample, close to 7 million persons in the 2006-based version and over 9 million records in a 2024 presentation — [StatCan Demosim methodology 2010](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/pub/91-551-x/2010001/meth-eng.htm); [Vezina, IMA 2024 slides](https://ima-2024.wifo.ac.at/_downloads/ad6f68ff7ca88e1bcff98cc6fc470da8/vezina.pdf); [StatCan Demosim overview 2025](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/17-20-0001/172000012025001-eng.htm)

### Inferences
- Cell-count arithmetic **(estimate)**: single-year age (0-100) x 2 sexes = 202 cells per country. x 50 regions = 10,100. x 4 education levels = 40,400. x 8 labour/occupation states = 323,200. x 10 income deciles = 3.2 million. x 5 household types = 16 million cells, i.e. more cells than a 1:10 agent sample of a 67M country, with most cells empty or fractional. This is the curse of dimensionality: the cell table grows multiplicatively per attribute while a sample of agents grows only linearly (one extra column per attribute).
- A pure age x sex x region cohort table is tiny (about 10^4 doubles = 80 KB) and a full monthly cohort-component step is of order 10^4-10^5 multiply-adds, i.e. microseconds **(estimate)**. It is the right tool for the head-count backbone, not for crossing opinions with income, education and household.
- **[BK]** Rogers' multiregional demography (1975) generalises the life table and Leslie matrix so each cohort carries a region (or any "state") and is moved by an origin-destination transition matrix per age/sex; multistate projection applies the same algebra to education, marital status or labour-force status. The Wittgenstein Centre/IIASA global projections by age x sex x education are the best-known multistate application. Not retrieved this session.
- **[BK]** National offices (ONS, US Census Bureau, INSEE, Destatis) all publish cohort-component national projections with single-year ages and published variant assumptions; these published assumption tables (ASFRs, mortality rates/improvement factors, net migration by age) are free to reuse and are exactly the inputs a game needs. Not retrieved this session.

## 2. Dynamic microsimulation models: what they simulate, sample sizes, languages, runtimes, alignment, weights, Monte Carlo variance

### Takeaway
Official dynamic microsimulation models run on samples of roughly 10^5 to 10^7 records (not 1:1 populations), mostly in annual discrete time, are tuned for policy accuracy rather than speed, and stay consistent with official projections through alignment; published runtimes are scarce, and where speed is mentioned it is "minutes to hours", so none is a ready-made engine for an interactive game.

### Cited Findings
- The standard surveys: Li & O'Donoghue (2013), "A survey of dynamic microsimulation models: uses, model structure and methodology", International Journal of Microsimulation 6(2):3-55, covering methodological choices in more than 60 models (base data, discrete vs continuous time, open vs closed, alignment, behaviour) — [IJM article 00082](https://microsimulation.pub/articles/00082); [UNU-MERIT working paper 2012-002](https://merit.unu.edu/publications/working-papers/abstract/?id=4640)
- O'Donoghue's follow-up survey (IJM 18(1), 2025) covers 2013-2023 and reports that nearly half of all papers in the field were published since the 2013 survey — [IJM article 00316](https://microsimulation.pub/articles/00316/figures)
- DYNASIM3 (Urban Institute, US; completed 2004) is based on the 1990-1993 SIPP panels with n = 100,000 persons (figure reported in IJM survey literature per search summary; exact page not pinned down) — [IJM survey](https://microsimulation.pub/articles/00082); [IJM, "Challenges and Opportunities of Dynamic Microsimulation Modelling"](https://microsimulation.pub/articles/00280)
- MINT (US SSA) forecasts retirement income distribution for the 1931-1960 birth cohorts, later extended to 1926-2018 cohorts; CBOLT (US CBO) is used to analyse entitlement reforms and the long-term fiscal outlook — [IJM survey table](https://microsimulation.pub/articles/00082)
- MIDAS (Belgium, Federal Planning Bureau) runs on about 553,000 individuals and is built with LIAM2 — [MIDAS presentation, Flemish government](https://assets.vlaanderen.be/image/upload/v1698132727/MIDAS_qhmpnm.pdf)
- LIAM2 is an open-source tool for discrete-time dynamic microsimulation developed mainly at the Belgian Federal Planning Bureau; its JASSS paper (2014) reports it is considerably faster and handles much larger datasets than its predecessor LIAM (no absolute timing retrieved) — [JASSS 17(3) 9](https://www.jasss.org/17/3/9.html); [LIAM2 documentation](https://liam2.readthedocs.io/en/stable/intro.html)
- SimPaths (Centre for Microsimulation and Policy Analysis, Essex) is an open-source life-course model coded in Java on the JAS-mine discrete-event libraries; country versions exist for the UK, Greece, Hungary, Italy and Poland; EU versions can be estimated from EU-SILC alone; individuals are nested in benefit units nested in households; reference paper IJM 18(1):95-133 (2025) — [IJM article 00318](https://microsimulation.pub/articles/00318); [SimPaths documentation](https://centreformicrosimulation.github.io/SimPaths); [model description](https://centreformicrosimulation.github.io/SimPaths/overview/model-description)
- DESTINIE 2 (INSEE, France; in use since 2010, mainly for long-term pension projections) starts from a representative sample from the Patrimoine 2009-2010 wealth survey; INSEE states that "the modest size of the sample allows for rapid simulation and thus the creation of numerous variants, at the cost of lower accuracy of results"; core in C++ with an R library; source public on GitHub — [INSEE, The Destinie 2 microsimulation model](https://www.insee.fr/en/information/8659672); [COR document](https://www.cor-retraites.fr/sites/default/files/2020-03/Doc%205_Destinie_INSEE.pdf)
- Statistics Canada LifePaths simulates "a large sample of detailed individual life courses" as "a set of mutually independent cases" (case-based, continuous-time, written in Modgen), is described as running "very efficiently on standard PCs", and is now discontinued; Modgen's on-the-fly tabulation reduces memory; Statistics Canada maintains a fork of OpenM++ (compiler, runtime, UI) — [StatCan LifePaths overview](https://www.statcan.gc.ca/en/microsimulation/lifepaths/overview); [StatCan microsimulation index](https://www.statcan.gc.ca/en/microsimulation/index)
- Demosim (Statistics Canada) runs on 7 to 9+ million census records (see section 1) — [StatCan](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/pub/91-551-x/2010001/meth-eng.htm)
- Static versus dynamic ageing: static ageing moves the sample to a future date by reweighting and uprating; dynamic ageing changes the records themselves; dynamic models carry a considerably heavier data load but can produce life histories — [Dekkers 2015, IJM 8(1):97-109](https://www.microsimulation.org/IJM/V8_1/5_IJM_8_1_Dekkers.pdf)
- Reweighting (static ageing) can come to rely excessively on small groups in volatile periods (Klevmarken 1997, as cited) — [JASSS 17(4) 12](https://www.jasss.org/17/4/12.html)
- Weights in dynamic models: expanding a weighted survey into unit-weight clones is costly because dynamic-ageing models already use a lot of memory; Dekkers & Cumpston (2012) propose treating the weight as just another variable carried by the record (URL-to-paper mapping by best match) — [IJM article 00072](https://microsimulation.pub/articles/00072)
- Static-ageing/alignment techniques are compared in a Finnish case study by Immervoll et al. — [EUROMOD Working Paper EM7/05](https://www.iser.essex.ac.uk/research/publications/working-papers/euromod/em7-05.pdf)
- A demographic microsimulation with an integrated household alignment method (keeping simulated households consistent with external household projections) — [arXiv 2006.09474](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.09474)

### Inferences
- Time treatment: LIAM2-family and SimPaths-style models are discrete-time (typically annual); Modgen/OpenM++ models (LifePaths, Demosim) are continuous-time with competing-risk event queues per case. For a game with a fixed monthly/quarterly tick, discrete time with monthly hazards (p_month = 1 - (1 - p_year)^(1/12)) is the natural fit and vectorises trivially; continuous time buys realism in event ordering at the price of per-agent event queues and poorer SIMD/cache behaviour.
- Sample-size precedent: 10^5 (DYNASIM3), about 5.5 x 10^5 (MIDAS), "modest" (DESTINIE 2), 7-9 x 10^6 (Demosim). A game sample of 10^5-10^6 weighted agents is therefore squarely inside official practice; 1:1 national populations are not used by statistical offices.
- Monte Carlo variance **(estimate, standard binomial arithmetic)**: for a share p estimated from n equally weighted agents, SE = sqrt(p(1-p)/n). At p = 0.3: n = 100 gives 4.6 pp; n = 1,000 gives 1.45 pp; n = 10,000 gives 0.46 pp; n = 100,000 gives 0.14 pp. With unequal weights use the Kish effective sample size n_eff = (sum w)^2 / sum(w^2) **[BK]**. A 1:100 sample of a 67M country gives about 1,000 agents per 100k-person constituency, so a sampled constituency vote share has roughly +/-3 pp 95% noise, too noisy for marginal FPTP seats unless noise is removed by construction.
- Variance-reduction levers a game can use that official models use less: (a) accumulate expected values (each agent contributes its probability vector times its weight) instead of sampling discrete outcomes for votes/opinions; (b) alignment, which forces event counts to equal the cohort-table expectation and so removes aggregate Monte Carlo noise in births, deaths, migration and employment; (c) common random numbers keyed on (seed, agent id, tick, process) so policy A versus policy B differ only through the policy **[BK for counter-based RNG such as Philox/Threefry]**.
- **[BK]** Alignment methods catalogued in the literature (Li & O'Donoghue, "Evaluating binary alignment methods in microsimulation models", JASSS 2014): multiplicative scaling, sidewalk, sorting by predicted probability, sorting by the difference between logistic-adjusted probability and a random number (SBD/SBDL), central-limit methods; the sorting-based variants hit the target count exactly. Not retrieved this session.
- **[BK]** PENSIM2 (UK DWP), CBOLT and MINT sample sizes and typical "hours per run" figures were not retrieved; treat any number for them as unknown.

### Gaps
- No published absolute runtime was retrieved for SimPaths, LIAM2, MIDAS, DESTINIE 2, DYNASIM, MINT, CBOLT, PENSIM2, LifePaths/OpenM++, or neworder; the LIAM2 JASSS paper has a performance section that should be read directly.
- No microsimulation-specific source on the number of agents needed for stable regional/subgroup aggregates was retrieved; the figures above are textbook sampling arithmetic.
- JAS-mine and neworder (Python/C++/MPI) framework details and the IMA literature beyond the two surveys were not retrieved.

## 3. Synthetic population generation: methods, tools, ready-made populations, licences, required marginals

### Takeaway
Believable synthetic countries are routinely built from public census marginals plus a small seed microdata sample using IPF/IPU or combinatorial optimisation, with Bayesian-network, copula and deep generative methods used when many attributes must be crossed; ready-made open populations now exist for the whole world (GLOPOP-S), the US (RTI) and England (SPC), but licence terms need checking per dataset.

### Cited Findings
- Method families: conventional (IPF, IPU, combinatorial optimisation, Markov/MCMC-based, fitness-based) versus deep generative (VAE, GAN and variants) — [arXiv 2211.13339, "Robustness Analysis of Deep Learning Models for Population Synthesis"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.13339)
- IPF cannot treat categorical and continuous variables alike, which motivated MCMC simulation-based synthesis; IPU fits household-level and person-level controls simultaneously by iteratively updating household weights — [arXiv 2211.13339](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.13339)
- Bayesian networks for population synthesis were introduced by Sun & Erath (2015); Borysov et al. used a variational autoencoder to fit the full joint distribution in high dimensions and compared it with a Gibbs sampler and a Bayesian network on Danish trip-diary data — [arXiv 1808.06910, "Scalable Population Synthesis with Deep Generative Modeling"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.06910v1)
- Bayesian networks, VAEs and GANs alone or combined with copulas have been evaluated for transferability across geographic levels (2023) — [arXiv 2302.09193](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.09193v3)
- A 2025 Winter Simulation Conference paper quantitatively compares five population synthesis techniques across spatial scale, country and dimensionality — [WSC 2025 paper inv184](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc25papers/inv184.pdf)
- GLOPOP-S (Ton et al., Scientific Data, Oct 2024): a global synthetic population for 2015 of about 2.0 billion households and 7.3 billion individuals consistent with admin-level-1 statistics; attributes are age, education, gender, income/wealth, settlement type, household size and household type (plus agricultural land ownership and dwelling characteristics for some Global South countries); built from Luxembourg Income Study and DHS microdata scaled to regional statistics; data on Harvard Dataverse (doi 10.7910/DVN/KJC3RH); the original LIS/DHS inputs are not published — [Scientific Data paper via PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11458621/); [IIASA repository record](https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/20024)
- RTI national synthetic population for the US (Scientific Data, 2025): reference year 2019, built from public 2015-2019 ACS 5-year estimates, 120,754,708 households and 303,128,287 individuals, spatially explicit, validation correlations with census variables mostly above r = 0.99, described as publicly available for research with code on GitHub — [RTI publication page](https://www.rti.org/publication/a-national-synthetic-populations-dataset-for-the-united-states); [RTI announcement](https://www.rti.org/announcements/new-rti-synthpop-dataset-groundbreaking-research)
- RTI offers the 2010 national/state synthetic population "for free for non-commercial use", and markets SynthPop as a turnkey dataset under negotiated commercial terms — [RTI U.S. Synthetic Household Population](https://www.rti.org/impact/rti-us-synthetic-household-populationtm); [RTI SynthPop](https://rti.org/impact/synthpop)
- Synthetic Population Catalyst (SPC, Alan Turing Institute; Salat et al. 2023): open-source tool producing a synthetic population of individuals for all of England at MSOA scale for 2020, with daily activity data; outputs per lieutenancy area are downloadable without running the code; documentation also mentions coverage of Wales and Scotland — [SPC documentation](https://alan-turing-institute.github.io/uatk-spc/); [paper](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/218733/1/salat-et-al-2023-synthetic-population-catalyst-a-micro-simulated-population-of-england-with-circadian-activities.pdf); [Urban Analytics Technology Platform](https://urban-analytics-technology-platform.github.io/projects/spc)

### Inferences
- Minimum public inputs for a believable synthetic country (synthesised from how GLOPOP-S, RTI and SPC are built): (1) region x age x sex counts; (2) household size and household type distributions by region; (3) education by age/sex; (4) labour status and occupation/industry by age/sex/region; (5) income distribution by household type/region (deciles or parametric); (6) one seed microdata file (public-use survey sample of a few thousand to a few hundred thousand records) to carry the within-household and cross-attribute correlations that marginals alone cannot. Everything except (6) is ordinary published census/LFS tabulation.
- For a game that ships data, licence is the binding constraint, not method: RTI's 2010 data are non-commercial only; GLOPOP-S's Dataverse licence was not confirmed (the article is CC BY, the dataset licence must be read on the Dataverse record); SPC's code/data licence must be read from its repository. Safer path: generate the game's own population at build time from openly licensed aggregate tables (Eurostat/national statistical office tables are generally reusable with attribution) with IPF/IPU, and ship only the generated agents plus the generator seed.
- IPF on a seed sample plus integerisation (or simply keeping fractional weights, which a weighted-agent engine can use directly) is sufficient for 6-10 attributes and is a few hundred lines of code; Bayesian-network/copula synthesis is the next step if opinion and wealth attributes must be imputed with realistic correlations. Deep generative synthesis is not needed for a game.
- **[BK]** Tools not retrieved this session: PopGen (ASU; IPU), PopulationSim (ActivitySim consortium; entropy-maximising list balancing), humanleague (Leeds; IPF and quasi-random integer sampling, used inside SPENSER/SPC), SPEW (CMU; synthetic ecosystems for many countries from IPUMS-International samples plus marginals), synthpop (R; CART-based synthesis for disclosure control, and the similarly named UrbanSim synthesizer), UrbanPop (ORNL), eqasim (open-data synthetic travel demand for France/Switzerland).

### Gaps
- Licence texts for GLOPOP-S (Dataverse), SPC and the 2019 RTI dataset were not confirmed.
- No EU-wide ready-made open synthetic population other than GLOPOP-S was confirmed; SPEW coverage/licence not retrieved.
- No source retrieved that states "which marginals are enough" in so many words; the list above is inferred from the documented inputs of existing datasets.

## 4. Large-scale agent-based simulation performance: agents, steps, hardware, and the techniques that deliver speed

### Takeaway
Published single-machine throughput for national-scale person simulators ranges from under 10^6 agent-steps per second per core (C with rich contact networks: OpenABM-Covid19) to about 7 x 10^6 (array-based NumPy/Numba: Covasim), while 1:1 national models with heavy interaction (JUNE, EpiCast, 1:1 economic ABMs) need clusters or tens of seconds to hours per step; the speed-ups that matter are array/tensor (struct-of-arrays) state, pre-allocation, weighted agents (rescaling), event-driven updates and partitioned parallelism, not the choice of ABM framework.

### Cited Findings
Epidemiological and urban simulators
- OpenABM-Covid19 (C core, Python/R interfaces via SWIG; UK-census-based default population of 1 million): about 3 s per simulated day for 1 million individuals on a 2015 MacBook Pro, needing 5 GB of memory, reduced to 1.7 GB with contact tracing disabled; speed and memory linear in population (tested 100k to 1M); memory pre-allocated at start. A later citing study quotes 1.5 s per day on a 2019 MacBook Pro — [medRxiv preprint 2020](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.09.16.20195925v1.full); [PLOS Computational Biology 2021](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pcbi.1009146)
- Covasim (pure Python with NumPy arrays and Numba): compute scales at roughly 7 million simulated person-days per second of single-core CPU time on an Intel i9-8950HK laptop (published version; the preprint said about 2 million; the FAQ and SciPy paper say 5-10 million); memory about 1 KB per agent (1 GB per million people); 100,000 agents for 500 days needs about 100 MB and 5-10 s; roughly linear scaling over three orders of magnitude; compute depends on agents and contacts per agent, not on number infected; speed attributed to "dynamic rescaling" and array-based computation — [PLOS Computational Biology 2021](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pcbi.1009149); [Covasim FAQ](https://docs.idmod.org/projects/covasim/en/latest/faq.html); [SciPy 2022 proceedings](https://proceedings.scipy.org/articles/majora-212e5952-00e)
- EpiSimdemics (Charm++, US national contact network): 280.4 million agents, 180 days, 655,360 cores of Blue Waters, 10.41 s total, 0.0578 s per simulated day; an earlier 2014 run is summarised as about 300 million people and 1.5 billion edges on about 352,000 cores, 120 days in about 12 s (secondary summary) — [Loimos paper comparison table (arXiv 2024)](https://pssg.cs.umd.edu/assets/papers/2024-01-loimos-arxiv.pdf); [CCGrid 2017 paper](https://www.sci.utah.edu/publications/Bha2017a/ccgrid2017a.pdf); [arXiv 1806.02578](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.02578)
- EpiCast: 281 million agents, 180 days, 256 cores, 8-12 hours total, about 160 s per simulated day — [Loimos comparison table](https://pssg.cs.umd.edu/assets/papers/2024-01-loimos-arxiv.pdf)
- EpiHiper: 288 million agents, 72,000 simulated days in total (many replicates), 6,400 cores on Bridges-2, 32 h 42 min, listed as about 0.141 s per day (the row's arithmetic is not self-consistent as summarised, so verify in the source) — [Loimos comparison table](https://pssg.cs.umd.edu/assets/papers/2024-01-loimos-arxiv.pdf)
- JUNE (England, 1:1): each of about 53 million people assigned to households, workplaces, schools; the England run needs about 100 GB of RAM, was run on 32-core/128 GB nodes, and a calibration run takes "just over 10 hours" after moving to HPC — [JUNE developer write-up](https://htruong0.github.io/projects/june); [medRxiv paper](https://medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.12.15.20248246v2.full.pdf)
- CityCOVID (Argonne; ChiSIM on Repast HPC, C++/MPI): 2.7 million individuals moving among 1.2 million places on hourly schedules; requires HPC — [Gordon Bell finalist justification 2021](https://www.mcs.anl.gov/~wozniak/papers/GB_2021.pdf); [Argonne model description](https://www.anl.gov/dis/citycovid-about-the-model)
- MATSim: the default QSim processes every link holding at least one agent at every time step, which bottlenecks at millions of agents; an SBB study on a 10% Switzerland scenario found per-iteration runtime stops improving after about 8 threads and extrapolated a 100% Switzerland run of 1,000 iterations to about 116 days with QSim; Hermes is an event-driven drop-in replacement adopted by SBB, after which replanning dominates runtime — [Bruno et al., STRC 2019](https://www.strc.ch/2019/Bruno_EtAl.pdf); [Graur et al., Hermes paper 2021](https://www.dpss.inesc-id.pt/~rbruno/papers/dgraur-abmtrans21.pdf)

Economic 1:1 models
- An MPI implementation of the Poledna et al. agent-based macro model takes around 20 s per iteration for a 1:1 model of Austria (about 10 million agents) — [ICCS 2019 paper (DOI 10.1007/978-3-030-22741-8_30)](https://unpaywall.org/10.1007%2F978-3-030-22741-8_30)
- Gill, Lalith, Poledna, Hori, Fujita & Ichimura (IEEE TPDS, 2021): hybrid MPI + OpenMP implementation simulates a full economic model with 331 million agents within 108 s using 128 CPU cores at 70% strong scalability (what the 108 s spans, one period or a full run, is not clear from the summary) — [IIASA repository](https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/17104)
- Axtell, "120 Million Agents Self-Organize into 6 Million Firms: A Model of the U.S. Private Sector" (2016): a 1:1 model of the US private-sector workforce; runtime not retrieved — [bibliographic record](https://researchr.org/publication/Axtell16/related)

General frameworks
- AgentTorch / "Large Population Models" (MIT, Chopra et al., arXiv July 2025): for an 8.4 million-agent New York City population, simulation time falls from about 50 hours (conventional ABM) to about 5 minutes (600x), calibration from 100,000 hours to 20 minutes, analysis from 5,000 hours to 10 s; the paper attributes this to tensorised execution and composable interactions and says millions of agents run on commodity hardware (exact hardware not retrieved); these are the authors' own figures, not independently verified — [arXiv 2507.09901](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09901); [earlier AAMAS 2025 paper, arXiv 2409.10568](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.10568v2)
- FLAME GPU 2 (Richmond et al., Software: Practice and Experience 53(8):1659-1680, 2023): CUDA framework; benchmarks use models with millions of agents, report 3.5x and 10x gains for ensembles/concurrent execution over a baseline GPU implementation, and a Sugarscape model with up to 16 million agents; absolute step times not retrieved — [White Rose repository](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/199416/); [Sheffield RSE benchmark blog 2023](https://rse.shef.ac.uk/blog/2023-08-18-benchmarking-flamegpu2-on-h100-a100-and-v100-gpus)

Game precedent
- Victoria 3 (Paradox): a tick is 6 in-game hours with work split across yearly/monthly/weekly/daily ticks; the performance driver is the number of Pops (cohort cells), not population size; migration splits Pops and so raises Pop count and degrades late-game performance, and the 1.6 patch reworked migration to avoid splitting; a main speed-up was parallelising modifier calculations — [Dev Diary 76: Performance](https://www.paradoxinteractive.com/games/victoria-3/news/dev-diary-76-performance); [Dev Diary 105: Migration in 1.6](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/threads/victoria-3-dev-diary-105-migration-in-1-6.1624226/page-6)

### Inferences
- Normalised throughput **(estimate from the cited figures)**: OpenABM-Covid19 about 0.3-0.7 million agent-days per second on one laptop core (including per-contact transmission and contact-tracing memory; contacts per agent not retrieved); Covasim about 7 million per core-second; JUNE about 53M agents x roughly 100-200 simulated days in 10 h on 32+ cores, i.e. order 10^4 agent-days per core-second (Python object graph); EpiCast 281M/160 s/256 cores, about 7 x 10^3 per core-second; Gill et al. 331M/108 s/128 cores, about 2.4 x 10^4 agent-updates per core-second if the 108 s is one period; Austria 1:1 about 5 x 10^5 agents per second of wall time. The three-orders-of-magnitude spread is explained by interaction cost (contacts, market matching, message passing), not by agent count.
- A population/opinion step in a political game has almost no agent-to-agent interaction (transitions depend on the agent's own state plus a handful of global/regional variables), so it sits at or above the Covasim end of the range, and a compiled struct-of-arrays implementation should beat Covasim's NumPy/Numba figure by an order of magnitude (see section 9).
- Techniques ranked by evidence: (1) array/columnar state instead of objects (Covasim, mesa-frames, AgentTorch, Ark.jl ECS result); (2) agents carrying weights/"dynamic rescaling" so fewer agents represent the population (Covasim); (3) pre-allocated memory and index-based links (OpenABM-Covid19); (4) event-driven instead of every-entity-every-tick updates where activity is sparse (Hermes vs QSim); (5) keep the number of simulated entities bounded by merging identical cells (Victoria 3); (6) GPU/tensor execution for 10^6-10^7 agents (FLAME GPU 2, AgentTorch), useful but it adds a hardware dependency and cross-vendor determinism problems for a consumer game; (7) MPI/Charm++ partitioning is irrelevant on one PC.
- General-purpose ABM frameworks (NetLogo, Mesa, MASON, GAMA, Repast Simphony) are benchmarked at 10^3-10^5 agents and are 3-30x slower than Agents.jl, itself slower than an ECS; none is a candidate runtime for a shipped game. They are prototyping tools.

## 5. Opinion and voting models usable in a game; comparison with Democracy 3/4

### Takeaway
The cheap, empirically grounded route is a regression-style mapping from demographic cells or agents to opinions and vote probabilities (the MRP logic), combined with "fundamentals" terms (economy, incumbent approval, time in office); social-influence opinion-dynamics models are computationally cheap but poorly validated against data, so they should be a secondary drift term rather than the core. Democracy 3/4 already uses a weighted-membership version of this idea on about 2,000 sampled voters.

### Cited Findings
Democracy 3 / Democracy 4 (Positech)
- Democracy 3: each individual voter is a mixture of a subset of 21 voter groups; "a custom-designed neural network is used to model individual voters, each with varying memberships of voting groups, political parties and pressure groups" — [Steam store page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/245470/Democracy_3)
- Group membership drifts: income groups follow the economic simulation (policies push voters into middle-income or wealthy groups, bringing new attitudes); designer notes on capitalists growing under low taxes etc., with long-term effects deliberately sped up so players notice them; a "focus group" view exposes individual voters — [Cliff Harris blog, May 2013](https://www.positech.co.uk/cliffsblog/2013/05/05/changing-the-voters-opinions-long-term-changes/); [Cliff Harris blog, Feb 2013](https://www.positech.co.uk/cliffsblog/2013/02/22/democracy-3-voter-type-income-design/)
- Democracy 4: the electorate is modelled by 2,000 simulated voters; a voter may be in about 10 voting groups at different membership strengths; happiness is computed from inputs received through those groups — [Steam community gameplay guide](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=2242836711); [Cliff Harris blog, Mar 2022](https://www.positech.co.uk/cliffsblog/2022/03/02/on-the-visualization-of-voter-approval-distribution-in-democracy-4/)
- Vote rule: in the two-party logic a voter over 50% happiness votes for the player, otherwise for the opposition; with three parties only voters at 66%+ vote for the player; later versions add moving hidden thresholds for voting, sympathising, membership and activism that drift with public mood — [Cliff Harris blog, multi-party support, Nov 2019](https://www.positech.co.uk/cliffsblog/2019/11/19/democracy-4-multi-party-support/); [Cliff Harris blog, Apr 2022](https://www.positech.co.uk/cliffsblog/2022/04/08/yet-more-refinement-and-complexity-regarding-party-politics-in-democracy-4/)
- Turnout is a separate check depending on party membership, strength of feeling, the campaign effectiveness of the voter's usual party and an innate tendency to vote randomised at creation; voters have an age-based elasticity of opinion; protest votes were added later — [Cliff Harris blog, Oct 2020](https://positech.co.uk/cliffsblog/2020/10/21/modelling-voter-happiness-in-a-video-game-is-interesting-but-also-horrendous); [Cliff Harris blog, Oct 2021](https://www.positech.co.uk/cliffsblog/2021/10/25/protest-votes-in-democracy-4-final-piece-of-the-puzzle/)

MRP (demographic cells to opinions)
- YouGov's 2017 UK general election model used multilevel regression and post-stratification: about 50,000 panel interviews per week are used to fit vote intention for each "voter type"; the number of each type in every constituency is estimated from the ONS Annual Population Survey, the British Election Study and 2015/2016 vote results; the model pools information across similar voters in different constituencies; it called 93% of seats and the hung parliament correctly — [YouGov, "How the YouGov model for the 2017 general election works"](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/18266-how-yougov-model-2017-general-election-works); [YouGov 2019 model explainer (Lauderdale)](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/26533-how-yougovs-2019-general-election-model-works); [Lauderdale, Bailey, Blumenau & Rivers, IJF paper](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10074013/1/ijf_final_submission.pdf); [Gelman blog, June 2017](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2017/06/01/u-k-news-article-congratulates-yougov-using-modern-methods-polling-inference/)

Fundamentals / economic voting
- The election-forecasting literature has long centred on two factors, the economy and the incumbent's popularity; the negative effect of long tenure ("time for change", Abramowitz 1988) is described as one of the main lessons of the literature; voters attend mainly to the recent evolution of the economy (Lewis-Beck & Stegmaier) — [PS: Political Science & Politics, introduction to forecasting the 2024 US elections](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/3B0CD7678126F613C38AA011D3C034E2)
- Lewis-Beck et al. (International Journal of Forecasting 41(4):1655-1665, 2025) review political-economy forecasting models, defending their accuracy alongside lead time, parsimony and transparency, with extensions beyond the US/Europe — [IJF 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/intfor/v41y2025i4p1655-1665.html)

Opinion dynamics
- Model classes: continuous-opinion models (French-DeGroot, Friedkin-Johnsen, Abelson, Deffuant-Weisbuch, Hegselmann-Krause) and discrete models (the Axelrod model is mentioned among these); DeGroot averaging on a connected network converges to consensus, while bounded-confidence models average only with others within a confidence bound and produce clustering/fragmentation — [arXiv 2511.00401, "Opinion Dynamics: A Comprehensive Overview" (2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.00401); [arXiv 1909.12089, "From classical to modern opinion dynamics"](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.12089)
- Empirical validation is a recognised gap: the 2016 review "Opinion dynamics: models, extensions and external effects" notes a lack of validation against real data, and a 2024 JASSS study that calibrates a model to a survey experiment calls validation a major challenge — [arXiv 1605.06326 summary](https://emergentmind.com/papers/1605.06326); [JASSS 27(1) 17](https://www.jasss.org/27/1/17.html)

### Inferences
- Democracy 3/4's voter model is effectively: happiness_i = sum over groups g of membership_ig x groupApproval_g, then threshold to vote, plus a separate turnout draw. It is a linear random-effects model on fuzzy group memberships, evaluated on a 2,000-voter sample, with no geography, no household, no real head-counts and thresholds instead of a choice model. A new game can keep the same cheap algebra but replace fuzzy group memberships with real attributes and real weights.
- Recommended cheap core **(design inference)**: for each agent (or cell) i and party p, utility U_ip = partyID_ip + valence_p(economy, competence, leader, time in office) - sum_k salience_ik x (position_ik - partyPosition_pk)^2 + demographic intercepts; vote probabilities by multinomial logit (softmax) over parties plus an abstention option. This nests spatial proximity voting, valence/economic voting, issue salience and party identification in one expression; cost is K issues x P parties multiply-adds per agent (for K = 16, P = 8, about 128 fused multiply-adds, tens of nanoseconds with SIMD) **(estimate)**.
- MRP supplies the calibration recipe: estimate intercepts and slopes by demographic category from public election-study microdata, then post-stratify over the synthetic population. In engine terms an MRP model is just a table of coefficients per attribute level, so applying it to 10^6 agents is a handful of table look-ups per agent.
- Opinion-dynamics models are cheap if the interaction graph is replaced by group/region means: DeGroot or Friedkin-Johnsen with mean-field mixing ("each agent moves a fraction alpha toward the mean of its region x class x media-diet group, anchored to its initial position") is O(N) per tick; pairwise Deffuant or Hegselmann-Krause on an explicit social network is O(N x degree) and adds random memory access. Since the literature itself flags weak empirical validation, use social influence as a slow homogenising/polarising drift, with policy outcomes and economic conditions as the main drivers.
- Believability comes mostly from fundamentals: a model in which incumbent support responds to real income growth, unemployment and time in office reproduces the best-validated regularities of real elections; ideology and group interests then distribute that swing unevenly across cells.
- **[BK]** Classic references not retrieved this session: Downs (1957) spatial/proximity voting and the median voter result; Rabinowitz & Macdonald (1989) directional voting; Stokes (1963) valence issues; Campbell, Converse, Miller & Stokes (1960) "The American Voter" (Michigan model, party identification, funnel of causality); Fiorina (1981) retrospective voting; Hibbs "Bread and Peace"; Fair's presidential vote equation; Riker & Ordeshook (1968) calculus of voting (R = pB - C + D) for turnout; Axelrod (1997) culture dissemination; Deffuant et al. (2000); Hegselmann & Krause (2002); Laver & Sergenti (2012) agent-based party competition; Zaller (1992) receive-accept-sample model for media effects; Argyle et al. (2023) "silicon samples" (LLM-simulated respondents).

## 6. Electoral system mechanics to support cheaply

### Takeaway
No sources could be retrieved for this question before the search budget ran out; everything below is unverified background knowledge. In engine terms all common electoral formulas are trivial post-processing of a (constituency x party) vote matrix and cost microseconds.

### Cited Findings
- None retrieved in this session (search budget exhausted before the electoral-systems and coalition-theory queries ran).

### Inferences
- **[BK]** FPTP: per constituency, the seat goes to argmax of party votes; cost O(C x P) (e.g. 650 x 8).
- **[BK]** Highest-averages PR: repeatedly award a seat to the party with the largest votes / divisor(seats already won); D'Hondt divisors 1, 2, 3, ...; Sainte-Lague 1, 3, 5, ...; modified Sainte-Lague raises the first divisor (1.4 classically). Largest-remainder methods use a quota (Hare = votes/seats, Droop) then allocate leftover seats by largest remainders. Cost O(S log P) with a heap.
- **[BK]** Thresholds are a filter before allocation (national or district level, sometimes with constituency-seat exemptions as in Germany); mixed-member systems are FPTP plus a compensatory PR tier; two-round systems need a second vote-choice evaluation restricted to the qualified candidates, which a utility-based voter model gives for free (recompute softmax over the remaining options, including abstention).
- **[BK]** Preferential systems (AV/IRV, STV) need rankings; with a utility model, rankings are obtained by sorting utilities per agent or, more cheaply, by iteratively eliminating and recomputing choice probabilities over the remaining set per cell.
- **[BK]** Coalition formation: Riker's minimal winning coalitions (no surplus member), minimum-size variants, Axelrod's minimal connected winning (ideologically adjacent on one dimension), de Swaan's minimal range/policy distance, Gamson's law for portfolio shares proportional to seat contribution, Laver & Shepsle's portfolio-allocation model. With P up to about 12 parliamentary parties, exhaustively enumerating all 2^P subsets (4,096) and scoring each on majority, surplus, ideological range and inter-party relations is trivial.
- **[BK]** Disproportionality can be reported with the Gallagher least-squares index; effective number of parties with Laakso-Taagepera.
- Engine implication: geography is the only real requirement electoral systems place on the population model. Every agent/cell needs a constituency (or district) index, so vote tallies are a weighted scatter-add into a (district x party) matrix; with expected-value voting this is deterministic.

### Gaps
- All formula details, real-world threshold values and the empirical performance of coalition theories (e.g. Martin & Stevenson-style tests) need sourcing; nothing was verified in this session.

## 7. Households, labour-market transitions, income and wealth with real data: accessible sources and parametric shortcuts

### Takeaway
Aggregated tables are openly reusable and Eurostat now publishes free public-use microdata files for EU-SILC and EU-LFS, but the richer scientific-use microdata (EU-SILC/LFS SUFs, and by reputation HFCS, LIS, IPUMS) are restricted to recognised research use, so a commercial game should calibrate from published tables and public-use files rather than embed restricted microdata.

### Cited Findings
- Eurostat public-use files (PUFs) for EU-LFS and EU-SILC are free, open-access, de-identified microdata that no longer require registration — [EUI library guide to Eurostat public microdata](https://eui.eu/Research/Library/ResearchGuides/Economics/Statistics/DataPortal/EurostatPublicUse)
- Eurostat scientific-use files are released only to recognised research entities under Commission Regulation (EU) No 557/2013, for scientific purposes; the EU-LFS DOI record describes the dataset as available to eligible researchers — [Eurostat/CROS, Access to microdata for scientific purposes](https://cros.ec.europa.eu/node/1402); [Eurostat EU-LFS DOI record 2024](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/documents/203647/20621087/EU+LFS+DOI+2024.pdf)
- Official models show which sources carry which processes: SimPaths estimates all its dynamics (household formation, labour, income) from EU-SILC for EU countries — [IJM article 00318](https://microsimulation.pub/articles/00318); DESTINIE 2 starts from the French wealth survey (Patrimoine) — [COR/INSEE](https://www.cor-retraites.fr/sites/default/files/2020-03/Doc%205_Destinie_INSEE.pdf); DYNASIM3 from SIPP panels — [IJM survey](https://microsimulation.pub/articles/00082); Demosim from the census 20% sample — [StatCan](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/pub/91-551-x/2010001/meth-eng.htm)
- GLOPOP-S shows that LIS and DHS microdata can be used to derive an openly published synthetic population, while the underlying LIS/DHS files themselves cannot be redistributed — [Scientific Data 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11458621/)
- The RTI US population is built entirely from public ACS 5-year estimates — [RTI](https://www.rti.org/publication/a-national-synthetic-populations-dataset-for-the-united-states)
- Political calibration data used by YouGov's MRP: ONS Annual Population Survey, British Election Study, past constituency results — [YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/18266-how-yougov-model-2017-general-election-works)

### Inferences
- Practical split for a small team: (a) ship only derived parameters (transition matrices, regression coefficients, distribution parameters) and a synthetic population generated from open tables; (b) use restricted or non-commercial microdata, if at all, only offline for estimation where the licence permits publishing derived statistics. Whether a commercial studio qualifies for any research-only source is a legal question to settle per dataset.
- **[BK]** Access conditions not retrieved this session: ECB HFCS microdata are released on application for scientific, non-commercial research; LIS/LWS microdata are queried remotely through LISSY (no download) by registered non-commercial users; IPUMS (International, USA, CPS) is free on registration but restricts redistribution of microdata and, for IPUMS-International, restricts use to scholarly/educational purposes. US public-use microdata (ACS PUMS, CPS, SIPP, SCF) are public domain and are the least encumbered source of person/household microdata.
- **[BK]** Parametric shortcuts in common use: lognormal (or Singh-Maddala/GB2) body with a Pareto upper tail for income, Pareto tail with alpha about 1.5 for wealth versus about 2-3 for income; wealth as an age-profiled multiple of permanent income plus a heavy-tailed residual; labour-market status as a first-order Markov chain (employed / unemployed / inactive / education / retired) with monthly transition matrices by age x sex x education, scaled each tick so the aggregate unemployment rate matches the macro model (alignment); household formation/dissolution as annual hazards by age/sex (partnership, separation, leaving home, births by parity); earnings as fixed effect + AR(1) shock on log earnings. Labour Force Survey flow statistics (published quarterly by Eurostat and national offices) give the transition matrices directly.
- Storing income and wealth as rank/quantile position within the agent's reference group (1-2 bytes) and mapping rank to currency through a parametric distribution owned by the macro model keeps the micro and macro layers consistent by construction and makes inflation/growth free.

## 8. Hybrid cohort + weighted-sample designs: precedent and practical guidance

### Takeaway
The hybrid is standard official practice under other names: a cohort-component projection supplies the authoritative totals and a weighted micro-sample is dynamically aged and aligned (or statically reweighted) to those totals; published evidence that a microsimulation can reproduce a cohort-component model exactly, and that agents can carry and change weights, supports a game design of cohort-table backbone plus 10^5-10^6 weighted agents.

### Cited Findings
- Two ways to move a sample through time: static ageing (reweight and uprate) and dynamic ageing (change the records); the choice depends on purpose and data, and dynamic models have the heavier data load — [Dekkers 2015, IJM 8(1)](https://www.microsimulation.org/IJM/V8_1/5_IJM_8_1_Dekkers.pdf)
- Static ageing is defined by Immervoll et al. as methods that align micro-data with other known (aggregate) information, with a comparison of techniques — [EUROMOD WP EM7/05](https://www.iser.essex.ac.uk/research/publications/working-papers/euromod/em7-05.pdf)
- Static reweighting can over-rely on small groups when conditions change sharply (Klevmarken, as cited) — [JASSS 17(4) 12](https://www.jasss.org/17/4/12.html)
- Weights in dynamic-ageing models can be handled by treating the weight as a variable on the record instead of cloning records — [IJM article 00072 (Dekkers & Cumpston 2012; mapping by best match)](https://microsimulation.pub/articles/00072)
- Macro (cohort-component) projections can be re-implemented as microsimulations producing identical results and then extended — [IHSN DYNAMIS-POP](https://documentation.ihsn.org/dynamis/DYNAMIS-POP-MRT-2017_v01_01_Chapter1.html); worked example for India — [Marois & KC 2021](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-030-79111-7.pdf)
- But naive micro designs can drift from the cohort benchmark, by up to 20% in one India comparison, which is why alignment is used — [IJM article 00289](https://microsimulation.pub/articles/00289)
- Household-level alignment integrated into a demographic microsimulation — [arXiv 2006.09474](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.09474)
- Trading sample size for speed is an explicit official choice: DESTINIE 2's "modest" sample enables rapid simulation of many variants at lower accuracy — [INSEE](https://www.insee.fr/en/information/8659672)
- Agents representing a varying number of real people: Covasim's "dynamic rescaling" lets one agent stand for many people and is credited (with array computation) for its laptop-scale performance — [Covasim, PLOS Comput Biol 2021](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pcbi.1009149)
- Cohort-cell games hit a ceiling when cells fragment: Victoria 3's cost scales with Pop count and migration-driven splitting degraded late-game speed — [Victoria 3 Dev Diary 105](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/threads/victoria-3-dev-diary-105-migration-in-1-6.1624226/page-6)
- A sampled-voter game precedent: Democracy 4 uses 2,000 simulated voters — [Steam community guide](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=2242836711)

### Inferences
- Proposed hybrid **(design inference)**: Layer A, a cohort table (age x sex x region, about 10^4 cells) advanced by cohort-component arithmetic, owning head-counts, births, deaths and migration deterministically. Layer B, N weighted agents grouped in households, owning the joint distribution of everything else (education, occupation, labour status, income/wealth rank, opinions, party attachment). Each tick: age agents; apply agent-level transitions; then align, i.e. rescale agent weights (static-ageing style raking to the Layer A cell totals) or select events so counts match (dynamic alignment). Deaths and emigration can be expressed as weight decay rather than agent deletion, and births/immigration as weight added to recycled newborn/immigrant agents, which keeps N constant and memory static.
- Weight handling: pure reweighting drifts toward a few heavy agents (the Klevmarken problem); periodic resampling (split heavy agents, merge/drop light ones, as in particle filters) keeps n_eff close to N **[BK for the particle-filter analogy]**. Monitor n_eff per region.
- Sample-size guidance **(estimate)**: choose N from the smallest unit whose results the player sees as "real". National PR result only: 10^4-10^5 agents suffice (SE under 0.5 pp). 50 regions with regional parties: 10^5-5 x 10^5. 650 FPTP constituencies: about 10^3 agents each (about 6.5 x 10^5 total) is adequate only with expected-value voting; with sampled discrete votes one would need about 10^4 per constituency (6.5 x 10^6) to get below +/-1 pp. Stratify the sample by constituency (equal agents per seat, unequal weights) rather than sampling proportionally.
- A 1:1 population (10^7-10^8 agents) adds no statistical information the game can use once outcomes are computed as expectations; its only benefits are narrative ("every citizen exists") and exact small-area counts, at 10-100x the memory and time (section 9).
- Avoid the Victoria 3 failure mode: never let cells or agents multiply through splitting; fixed N with weight transfer keeps cost constant across a 50-year game.

### Gaps
- No published example of a game using cohort tables plus an aligned weighted agent sample was found; the design is assembled from official-statistics precedent.
- No retrieved source quantifies weight degeneracy in long static-ageing runs.

## 9. Memory and compute sizing on a 2024-2026 consumer PC

### Takeaway
On cited evidence, 10^5-10^6 weighted agents fit in tens of megabytes and can be stepped in milliseconds to tens of milliseconds; 10^7 is feasible (hundreds of megabytes, roughly 0.1-1 s per step multithreaded) and 10^8 is possible in RAM terms but is memory-bandwidth-bound at seconds per step. All per-step figures below are estimates anchored on the cited Covasim and OpenABM-Covid19 throughputs.

### Cited Findings
- Covasim: about 7 x 10^6 simulated person-days per second per core in NumPy/Numba (Intel i9-8950HK laptop CPU), about 1 KB of RAM per agent; 100,000 agents x 500 days in 5-10 s — [PLOS Comput Biol 2021](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pcbi.1009149); [Covasim FAQ](https://docs.idmod.org/projects/covasim/en/latest/faq.html)
- OpenABM-Covid19: 1 million agents at 1.5-3 s per simulated day single-threaded on 2015/2019 MacBook Pros; 5 GB (5 KB per agent) with contact-tracing history, 1.7 GB (1.7 KB per agent) without; linear scaling — [medRxiv 2020](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.09.16.20195925v1.full)
- JUNE: about 100 GB RAM for about 53 million agents (about 1.9 KB per agent) and 10+ hours per run on 32-core nodes — [JUNE write-up](https://htruong0.github.io/projects/june)
- 1:1 economic ABMs: about 20 s per iteration for about 10 million agents (Austria, MPI); 331 million agents in 108 s on 128 cores — [ICCS 2019](https://unpaywall.org/10.1007%2F978-3-030-22741-8_30); [Gill et al. 2021](https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/17104)
- Columnar Python at scale: mesa-frames 1M agents 3.55 s and 10M agents 20.8 s on a wealth-exchange model (third-party benchmark, step count unknown) — [AMBER benchmarks](https://ambr.readthedocs.io/en/stable/benchmarks.html)
- An archetype ECS (Ark.jl) runs the Wolf-Sheep benchmark 3-7x faster than Agents.jl, which is itself 3-10x faster than Mesa/NetLogo/MASON in the same table — [ABMFrameworksComparison](https://github.com/JuliaDynamics/ABMFrameworksComparison)
- AgentTorch: 8.4 million agents simulated in about 5 minutes with tensorised execution — [arXiv 2507.09901](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09901)
- Democracy 4 runs its whole electorate as 2,000 voters — [Steam community guide](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=2242836711)

### Inferences
- Bytes per agent, compact struct-of-arrays layout **(estimate)**: age in months (u16, 2), sex + flags (1), region/constituency (u16, 2), education (1), occupation/sector (1), labour status + tenure (2), income rank (u16, 2), wealth rank (u16, 2), household id (u32, 4), household role (1), health/other (1), party identification + strength (2), turnout propensity (1), 16 issue positions as i8 (16), 8 issue saliences as u8 (8), weight (f32, 4) = about 50 bytes; allow 64 bytes with padding and scratch columns. Households add about 16-24 bytes per household (about 0.45 households per person). The 1-5 KB per agent of the epidemic models is dominated by contact networks and object overhead that a political model does not need.
- RAM **(estimate at 64 B/agent)**: 10^5 agents = 6.4 MB (fits in L2/L3 cache); 10^6 = 64 MB; 10^7 = 640 MB; 10^8 = 6.4 GB (needs a 16 GB machine and leaves little headroom for save-game snapshots, which at 10^8 are multi-GB even compressed).
- Per-step time **(estimate)**: a monthly step needs roughly 10-20 linear passes (ageing, mortality/migration weight updates, labour Markov transition, income update, household events at 1/12 annual hazard, opinion drift over 16 axes, 8-party utility + softmax, scatter-add into district tallies). Budget 30-100 ns per agent per step in compiled scalar code and 5-20 ns with AVX2 and 8 threads. That gives: 10^5 agents: 3-10 ms single-threaded, under 2 ms parallel. 10^6: 30-100 ms single-threaded, 5-20 ms parallel. 10^7: 0.3-1 s single-threaded, 50-200 ms parallel. 10^8: 3-10 s single-threaded, 0.5-2 s parallel and bounded by memory bandwidth (each pass over 6.4 GB at roughly 40-60 GB/s of dual-channel DDR5 costs 0.1-0.15 s **[BK for the bandwidth figure]**).
- Sanity check against cited figures: Covasim's 7 x 10^6 person-days per core-second is about 140 ns per agent-step in Python/Numba including contact-network transmission; OpenABM-Covid19's 1.5-3 microseconds per agent-day in C includes per-contact work and tracing memory (my reading of where its time goes; not profiled in the source). A contact-free, SoA, compiled step at 30-100 ns per agent is therefore plausible and consistent with these figures, but it is not itself a measured number.
- Quarterly versus monthly ticks only change the constant (3x fewer steps); a 1-second budget per turn is met with a 10x margin at 10^6 agents even single-threaded.
- AI/what-if cost matters more than the player's own tick: if the game runs look-ahead simulations (advisor forecasts, opposition AI, polling projections), each costs a full step sequence. At 10^6 agents a 48-month forecast is about 0.25-1 s parallel; at 10^7 it is 2.5-10 s. This argues for 10^5-10^6 agents plus an optional reduced-resolution clone (every k-th agent with weights x k) for forecasts.
- Reproducibility: fixed N, SoA arrays, integer/fixed-point state, expected-value tallies summed in a fixed order, and counter-based random numbers keyed by (seed, agent, tick, process) give bit-identical results regardless of thread count **[BK]**; GPU execution makes cross-vendor bit-identity much harder, another reason to prefer CPU SIMD for a small team.
- Recommendation implied by the evidence: hybrid representation with a cohort table backbone and about 2 x 10^5 to 10^6 household-structured weighted agents (roughly 1:50 to 1:300 for a 60-70M country), stratified by electoral district; treat 10^7 as a stretch "high detail" setting; do not target 1:1.

### Gaps
- No published benchmark of a compiled SoA demographic/opinion step on 2024-2026 desktop CPUs was retrieved; the nanosecond budgets are engineering estimates and should be validated with a one-day prototype (10^6 agents, 16 passes) before the architecture decision is frozen.
- No published SoA-versus-AoS or ECS iteration micro-benchmarks, and no memory-bandwidth reference, were retrieved (search budget exhausted).
- Save-file size and compression ratios for agent arrays were not researched.
